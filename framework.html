The Framework

How we decided which films belong — and why the canon looks the way it does.

A list of "great films" is only as useful as the thinking behind it. Anyone can copy the top 100 from a poll. What we've tried to build here is something more deliberate: a map of cinema's full range, from the first silent experiments to the blockbusters that rewired popular culture — organized around a clear set of principles.

This page explains those principles. What earns a film a place on the list. How we think about difficulty and accessibility. Why certain categories exist. And what we mean by a cinephile's education, as distinct from a simple best-of ranking.

The goal is not comprehensiveness for its own sake. It's to give any serious viewer — whether they're just starting out or filling the gaps in a lifetime of watching — a coherent path through the medium's history and possibilities.

The Tiered Approach

Three tiers of inclusion

Rather than a binary in-or-out decision, films are considered against a tiered framework. A film needs to clear only one bar in Tier 1 to belong. Tier 2 and Tier 3 entries require a stronger cumulative case.

I
Must-Include — any single qualifier
A film earns automatic inclusion if it appears in the top 50 of the Sight & Sound all-time poll, was directed by a filmmaker with undisputed master-level standing, or demonstrably changed the language of cinema itself. One qualifier is enough.
Examples: Tokyo Story, Citizen Kane, Breathless, The Passion of Joan of Arc
II
Include — two or more qualifiers
Films that appear on major critical lists, represent an essential genre or national cinema, won the Palme d'Or or an equivalent festival prize, or define a significant movement. Two of these five criteria earns a place.
Examples: Parasite, A Separation, The Wild Bunch, Chinatown
III
Deep Cuts — earned by context
Films a serious cinephile would know and value even without clearing the above bars. This tier allows for films with personal or cultural resonance — including the French cinema given particular weight here — and for the "Culture Definers" that shaped the broader landscape of film.
Examples: La Haine, Diva, Indiana Jones, Die Hard, Touki Bouki
The Qualifiers

What gets a film considered

These are the criteria a film is measured against. No single one is automatically disqualifying or automatically sufficient — they are weighed together.

📋
Critical List Consensus
Appears on three or more of the major canonical lists: Sight & Sound, AFI Top 100, Cahiers du Cinéma, Roger Ebert's Great Movies, or the They Shoot Pictures Don't They poll.
🎬
Master Director
Made by a filmmaker whose body of work is considered foundational to the medium — Bergman, Kubrick, Kurosawa, Fellini, Tarkovsky, Godard, Ozu, and their peers.
⚙️
Technical or Formal Innovation
Introduced a technique, approach, or formal idea that changed how films were made. Citizen Kane's deep focus. Breathless's jump cuts. Man with a Movie Camera's montage theory.
🏆
Major Festival Recognition
Won the Palme d'Or at Cannes, the Golden Lion at Venice, the Golden Bear at Berlin, or the Academy Award for Best Picture — particularly where this recognition proved durable over time.
🗺️
National Cinema Representation
Stands as the essential or defining work of a national film tradition that would otherwise be absent — Iranian cinema, Senegalese cinema, Taiwanese New Wave, Indian parallel cinema.
🌍
Cultural Impact
Shaped popular culture, audience expectations, or the business of cinema in a way that any educated viewer needs to understand — even if the film itself is not an "art" film by conventional standards.
🎭
Genre Definition
Represents the apex or the origin point of a genre — the western, the horror film, the noir, the science fiction epic — in a way that subsequent films in that genre consciously respond to.
⏳
Proven Durability
Films are generally given time to establish their reputation before inclusion. Very recent films are held to a higher bar — their presence on the list reflects unusually rapid and broad critical consensus.

Special Designations

Films that need a warning label

Some films on this list are formally or emotionally demanding in ways that set them apart. We flag these not to discourage viewing, but to calibrate expectations. A viewer who sits down expecting a conventional narrative experience and encounters Sátántangó unprepared is a viewer who may not give the film the attention it deserves.

💀
Demanding Watch
Long running times (3+ hours), unconventional narrative structures, deliberately slow pacing, or subject matter of unusual intensity. These films reward patience and preparation. Examples: Sátántangó (7h 18m), Jeanne Dielman (3h 21m), Come and See, Andrei Rublev, the Tarkovsky trilogy of Stalker, Solaris, and The Mirror.
🇫🇷
French Cinema
French film receives deeper coverage than other national cinemas, spanning from Renoir's pre-war masterpieces through the Nouvelle Vague to contemporary auteurs. This reflects both the outsized influence of French cinema on the medium globally, and a particular personal affinity for the tradition.
A Note on Culture Definers

Why Jaws belongs here

A cinephile's education that ignores popular cinema is an incomplete one. Films like Jaws, Star Wars, The Terminator, and The Dark Knight are not on this list despite being blockbusters — they're here because of what they did to the medium and to audiences.

Jaws invented the summer blockbuster and permanently altered Hollywood's release strategy. Star Wars redefined what special effects could do and created the template for franchise cinema. The Dark Knight proved that superhero films could sustain genuine moral complexity. These films changed the conditions under which all other films get made and seen. That's canonical by any reasonable definition.

The Culture Definers section makes no claim that these films are formally equivalent to Tokyo Story or Jeanne Dielman. It claims that understanding cinema means understanding what these films are and what they did.
